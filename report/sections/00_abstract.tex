\begin{abstract}
Music understanding systems often combine text and audio, and audio is often represented as
a graph: short segments become nodes, with edges linking segments that are close in time or
similar in content. When a graph's structure is a deterministic function of its own node
features, the data-processing inequality guarantees it cannot add Shannon information beyond
those features -- an analytic fact, not something an experiment can test. What experiments
can test is the practical question: whether such a graph still helps a real, finite model,
for instance as a computational shortcut or inductive bias. We test that practical question
directly, across four tasks built on the same node features and graph construction rule:
text-only tag prediction (no graph at all), graph-based genre classification,
graph-plus-text fusion, and audio-text contrastive retrieval. Tasks 2 and 4 each compare
their graph-based model against a width/depth-matched, lower-parameter, edge-free
alternative on held-out test data; Task 3's own direct comparison is validation-only, so its
evidence instead comes from two test-set diagnostics: branch-zeroing inside the trained
fusion model, and a cross-tabulation of independently-trained single-branch checkpoints. No
task shows a graph-based model with a \emph{learned} aggregation step clearly and reliably
ahead of its edge-free counterpart. One result complicates this and is reported rather than
hidden: a non-learned exact 2-hop neighbor aggregation in genre classification outperforms
GraphSAGE and both edge-free readings (though not the CNN baseline, which uses neither the
graph nor these node features) -- though it also uses a different training recipe (full-batch
optimization, no dropout, a different epoch budget) than the models it is compared against,
so this is suggestive of under-exploited topology, not a clean ablation. In genre classification, the
pooling-matched comparison between the two \emph{trained} models favors the edge-free
encoder. In fusion, zeroing the graph branch inside the trained model changes only 1.7\% of
individual (example, tag) predictions and costs 0.009 macro-F1: a small positive
contribution, not zero, just small. A separate complementarity check -- comparing the
independently-trained graph-only and text-only models -- at first appears to show the
graph-only model correcting 48.5\% of the text-only model's errors, but splitting these by
label polarity shows the graph-only model predicts a tag present at all for only 5 of 50
tags; fewer than 1\% of its apparent corrections are it actually detecting a tag the text
model missed, and the rest are it defaulting to "absent" while text wrongly said "present" --
this diagnostic, as originally framed, does not show real complementary signal. In
retrieval, a graph-based and a no-graph encoder show no reliable difference across three
training seeds, on every reported metric. We also checked two alternative explanations
rather than assume them away: caption text leaking target labels directly (it does,
substantially), and duplicate audio between train and test in one dataset (real, confirmed
for a small number of tracks, though its actual effect on any score was not separately
measured, and is left open as an unresolved caveat for the smallest comparison specifically). We do not conclude that graphs are useless for music -- only that this
specific similarity-based construction does not reliably help beyond its own node features,
for these tasks, on these datasets. We report this as the paper's central finding, not as a
failed architecture search.
\end{abstract}
